# Neighborhood Retail Case Study

Data and notes behind my article on neighborhood retail.

## About This Project

This is a student-led case study of a neighborhood retail shop (a "mart") in Nepal. The goal was to understand how a small business really runs day to day, rather than to build software for it. The repository holds the supporting data, analysis, and documentation behind my Medium article.

## Why I Did This

Instead of building software for a business, I wanted to understand how a real one actually works. My family's neighborhood mart has been part of its community for generations, and I used it as a living case study of how small-scale retail runs in practice: how stock is sourced, how goods are repackaged, and why the shop keeps certain products even when they earn little. I kept a personal observation log of daily visits and a separate log of bulk stock orders. This meant I never added to the workload of the shop owner or staff.

## Methodology

- **Daily visit log:** observations recorded in a spreadsheet during regular visits to the shop
- **Bulk stock order log:** a separate record of incoming bulk orders, including how items were repackaged
- **Approach:** observation only, with no surveys or extra work for staff

## Key Findings

1. **Repackaging is labor-heavy and low-reward.** Bulk staples such as rice, lentils, maida, salt, sugar, and cashews take one to three hours and several staff to repack, yet earn only low to moderate margins. Pre-packaged branded goods need no repackaging and earn high to very high margins.
2. **Necessity, not profit, decides what the shop stocks.** The owner knows repackaged staples are not very profitable, but they are daily necessities that customers depend on, so the shop keeps selling them.
3. **Supply, not demand, limits some products.** Gas sells with very low profit because of a supply shortage against high demand. This is a sourcing problem, separate from the labor pattern above.
4. **Trust is the shop's real asset.** The business runs on community relationships as much as on margins. When a customer once overpaid by mistake during a festival, the shop returned the excess in cash and absorbed the hassle itself.
